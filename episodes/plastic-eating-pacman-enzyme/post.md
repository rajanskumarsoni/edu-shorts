Can bacteria really eat plastic? Scientists at the University of Konstanz just discovered a revolutionary "Pac-Man enzyme" in forest soil that devours biodegradable plastics within a single year — but there's a shocking catch! 

Here is the first-principles scientific breakdown:
1. Soil bacteria produce a Family-VIII esterase enzyme anchored to their cell membranes.
2. It features an extra-wide active cleft nicknamed the "Pac-Man mouth" that chomps through polyester chains.
3. In soil tests, LCAP bioplastic was 100% devoured within ~280 days, releasing harmless CO2 and water.
4. The Shocking Twist: This exact same enzyme also cleaves Penicillin, mirroring bacterial antibiotic resistance!
5. The Golden Rule: Microbes cannot digest conventional polyethylene (HDPE); humans MUST design future plastics with hydrolysable biochemical breaking points!

Reference: Harry Lerner et al., The ISME Journal (2026), University of Konstanz.

### LinkedIn
Scientists at the University of Konstanz have uncovered an unexpected biological link between plastic degradation and antibiotic resistance.

Published in *The ISME Journal* (2026), researchers discovered a surface-anchored "Pac-Man" esterase (Family VIII) produced by soil bacteria that hydrolyzes biodegradable polyester (LCAP) films within a year, while conventional HDPE remained virtually untouched. 

More surprisingly, laboratory assays revealed that the enzyme also cleaves the beta-lactam ring of penicillin, shedding new light on how microbial enzymes evolve dual catalytic capabilities. 

Key takeaway for materials engineers: Microorganisms cannot magically break down inert C-C bonds. If we want biological recycling to solve the plastic crisis, we must synthesize polymers with hydrolysable biochemical breaking points.
